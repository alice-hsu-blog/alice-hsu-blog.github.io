[Auntie Reads the Red Chamber] When the False Is Taken for True, the True Becomes False Too: Chapters 41-60

July 15, 2026
1601 words
Categories:
Reading & Reflection

This post discusses plot details from Dream of the Red Chamber. If you’d rather avoid spoilers, please don’t read on.

Preface

Halfway through Dream of the Red Chamber! Once you commit to a serialized column, you realize keeping up with it really isn’t easy: I really want to fall behind on deadlines.

First, I want to add a bit to something I mentioned in the last installment: that the main throughline of Dream of the Red Chamber is watching Bao-yu and Dai-yu fall in love. I’m a little worried that gave readers the impression that Cao Xueqin spends huge stretches of the book on the two of them interacting directly, but that’s actually not quite true. At least across the first sixty chapters, Cao Xueqin spends a lot of time narrating the interactions of plenty of other characters too, weaving them into every link of the Jia family’s story, using these characters to display the full range of human experience, letting readers look down on the different directions different people choose to pursue in life.

Maybe, more than romantic love as a theme, what Cao Xueqin is really more focused on is the question of “what actually counts as a life worth pursuing.”

Quick recap of key plot points, Chapters 41-60

Xi-feng catches Jia Lian and Second Bao’s wife in bed together; Dai-yu and Bao-chai reconcile; Yuan-yang refuses, to the death, to marry Jia She; Xi-feng, recovering from a miscarriage, hands off household management to Lady Wang, Li Wan, and Tan-chun; Bao-yu is scared half out of his wits by a joke Zi-juan plays on him.

What actually counts as a life worth pursuing?

For every character in the story, the answer is different: for some it’s the purest form of love, for others it’s absolute power, for others it’s peaceful relationships. Whatever it is, the person pursuing it first has to assume that thing is real, not illusory. That premise has to hold before there’s any motivation to chase after it at all. And yet in Dream of the Red Chamber, Cao Xueqin deliberately blurs the line between what’s real and what’s illusory:

When the false is taken for true, the true becomes false too; where nothing is made to be something, something turns, in the end, again to nothing.

This couplet is what Jia Bao-yu reads early in the story during his dream-journey through the Land of Illusion, and it also serves as the central theme of the entire novel: illusion may well reflect reality, and what you believe to be real may well be false. Working from that premise, we find that the “illusory” dream sequences become the author’s tool for foreshadowing the “real” ending, for instance, the registers of the twelve beauties of Jinling that Bao-yu reads in his dream, whose verses actually describe the tragic fates awaiting several of the young women of the Grand View Garden. On the flip side, the “real” life depicted in the story ends up reading like a fleeting dream itself: scenes like everyone huddling together to compose poetry and grill meat together in the snow carry a sense of being almost too good to be real.

Even though readers may pick up on this, the characters within the story don’t: even after Bao-yu and Wang Xi-feng each experience prophetic dreams, neither of them really grasps what those dreams mean. Once they wake, they go right back to living in blissful ignorance of what’s coming, laughing as much as they want, crying as much as they want, chasing after whatever their hearts desire without restraint. For Bao-yu and Dai-yu, sincerity of feeling is what matters most, and they don’t care whether the rest of the world approves or not. For Wang Xi-feng, the image of the unbreakable powerhouse woman must be maintained at all costs: even after a miscarriage brought on by chronic overwork leaves her body severely weakened, she still puts up a strong front in front of others. For Lady Xing (Wang Xi-feng’s mother-in-law), her husband’s word is absolute law: even when her husband, Jia She, asks her to help him find a concubine, she does it without a moment’s hesitation.

Sounds pretty absurd, right? But maybe that’s exactly the effect Cao Xueqin was going for: using other people’s absurdity as a mirror, prompting us to ask ourselves whether the values we hold onto so tightly are actually real, and whether they’re really worth pouring everything we have into pursuing.

Is the ultimate truth of life really tragedy?

That said, it’s worth noting that this novel is, at its core, a tragedy: the dream sequences you assume are illusory turn out to foreshadow a genuinely tragic ending, while the beauty you assume is real ultimately amounts to nothing. Either way, beauty is never eternal, and illusion and emptiness are, in the end, what life’s truth really comes down to. Perhaps the author’s own lament on this is hidden in Dai-yu’s view on banquets:

Wherever people gather, they must eventually part; joyful as the gathering may be, isn’t the parting that follows bound to feel cold and desolate? And with that cold desolation comes sorrow, so wouldn’t it be better never to gather at all? Just like how flowers delight us when they bloom, and only bring us more heartache once they wither, so wouldn’t it be better if they never bloomed at all?

(Wait, doesn’t this basically boil down to: since you’re going to get hungry again eventually, wouldn’t it be better not to eat at all?)

As much as I like Dai-yu as a character, I don’t actually agree with her view here. Just as she believes gathering isn’t the whole of life, the sorrow of parting isn’t the whole of life either. The experience of people coming together is a lot like eating: sure, the meal eventually ends, but the food gets converted into nourishment (or, well, waste), bringing nourishment (or occasionally harm) that turns you into a slightly different person (hopefully healthier, or possibly just running to the bathroom more often).

And besides, if the ultimate truth of life really is tragedy and emptiness, then why do most of us still feel this urge to work hard, to long for good relationships, to chase after something? Is the belief that life is fundamentally tragic something we hold simply because we’ve been hurt? Just as Dai-yu’s view comes directly from having lost both her parents. Is a view shaped by pain automatically the absolute truth? If she had instead been born into a happy family, and had instead declared: precisely because gatherings and partings are part of life, we should cherish the present all the more, could that, too, have become just as valid a truth?

Our beliefs about the ultimate truth of life are shaped by our past experiences, and those beliefs, in turn, determine how we live our lives now.

I’ve wandered a bit far afield here. Even though I don’t agree with the author’s underlying view that “life is ultimately a tragedy,” I do think it’s genuinely interesting how, through these characters, he pushes readers to reconsider whether the lives we pursue are actually real and actually worth pursuing. Maybe we all need to stop and reconsider: behind the things we work so hard to chase in daily life, what values do they actually represent, and is there any chance those values are illusory too?

In Summary

The more I read, the more I find myself unable to resist the charm of every single character, even knowing full well the story is headed toward tragedy! It’s a feeling a lot like: you finally get close with everyone in your class, even the classmate you had a little friction with turns out to have her own lovable side, and then you realize graduation is just a year away QAQ.

Ugh, and now I’m starting to see the household servants stirring up trouble too. Could this be the first sign of the tragedy to come? To find out what happens next, stay tuned for the next installment.

[Fun Vocabulary]

Text in black is the definition, orange text is my own take on it.

  1. Baotan (爆炭, “exploding charcoal”): describes a fiery, hot temper. Example sentence: “That little wretch Qing-wen has a temper like exploding charcoal.” A wonderfully vivid image! You can practically picture Qing-wen’s temper crackling and popping like burning charcoal.
  2. “Opened a fruit stand”: beaten black and blue, skin broken and bleeding, like the riot of colors in a fruit stand’s produce. Example sentence: “Xiang-lian walked over to take a look, and realizing he (Xue Pan) wasn’t used to taking a beating, only used about thirty percent of his strength, giving his face a few slaps, and just like that, he’d ‘opened a fruit stand.’” A pretty funny metaphor: in modern terms, you’d basically say his face “opened up its own fruit shop."
  3. Hehezhezhe (蝎蝎螫螫): describes being excessively anxious, timid, and easily fussed.
  4. Shixin (“solid heart”): sincere, earnest. Example sentence: “Don’t go saying he (Bao-yu) is just some sincere, foolish child.” A pretty interesting descriptor: so does that mean if someone’s got a lot of twists and turns going on inside them, you could call them “hollow-hearted”? lol
