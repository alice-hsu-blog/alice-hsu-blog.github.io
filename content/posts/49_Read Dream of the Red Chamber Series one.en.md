## Preface
A while back, while reading Sabrina Huang's essays, I noticed she used some phrases that trace back to *Dream of the Red Chamber*, classical in origin yet surprisingly fresh. Something in me stirred, and I thought: why not take on the challenge of reading *Dream of the Red Chamber* myself?

I found the China Times Publishing edition of [*Dream of the Red Chamber*](https://www.kobo.com/tw/zh/ebook/9pDgkYMoDzmsRc-9GEdYSA?sId=b3198c0c-b5ac-4191-a7ea-169f775decc5&ssId=o7_yfR3w7oZpra6S8TRCS&cPos=1), with a preface by Pai Hsien-yung, and detailed annotations for every chapter. Since I'm reading the ebook version, clicking a hyperlink jumps straight between the footnotes and the main text, instead of the whole flipping-back-and-forth ordeal a print book would require. On top of that, some of Cao Xueqin's phrasing is fairly close to vernacular Chinese and isn't too hard to follow. All together, it's lowered the barrier to reading classical Chinese by quite a bit.

My original plan was to grind through all 120 chapters before writing up any thoughts, but that felt like way too much to take on at once: I was worried I'd lose all motivation to write by the time I got there. Then I remembered that the YouTuber JiuJiu Shoes once made a video series called "A Straight Guy Watches *Empresses in the Palace*," and I thought, why not borrow that concept and turn it into a similar serialized column? Watching this cast of teenage siblings fall in love, and these little brats trash-talking and brawling in the schoolroom, I realized I actually needed to take a deep breath and be patient about it, and I really felt like part of me had gotten old. Guess the column name has to be "Auntie Reads the Red Chamber" then. Recording my gut reactions and reflections as I read *Dream of the Red Chamber* should be pretty interesting.

### Quick recap of key plot points, Chapters 1-20
Lin Dai-yu arrives at the Jia household; Wang Xi-feng gets her revenge on Jia Rui; Ke-qing, Jia Rong's wife, passes away; Prospect Garden is completed; Yuan-chun returns home to visit her family (Dai-yu passes a note, and Bao-chai secretly feeds Bao-yu answers while helping him write poems).

## Jia Bao-yu
My first impression of Jia Bao-yu is that he's an extremely smooth-talking teenage brat. The very first thing he says to Lin Dai-yu is, "I've seen this sister somewhere before." Then he immediately gives her a nickname, "Frowner" (a very teenage-boy move, giving people nicknames!). When he's first introduced, there's a passage describing him as wayward and perverse in temper, reckless of what the world might think of him, which honestly makes him sound like a bit of a scoundrel: he lazes around all day and isn't much for studying, but he's incredibly popular with women, because he's endlessly sweet and attentive to every single one of them, always calling them "dear sister" or "dear cousin."

Even though my first impression of Bao-yu wasn't great, he does have a certain strange charm: he gives off the sense that he'd hand you his whole heart at any given moment. He doesn't bother with propriety or rules, and his feelings are completely unfiltered. For instance: he doesn't want his maid Aroma to go home, so he whines and clings to her over and over; when his friend Qin Zhong dies (even though Qin Zhong wasn't exactly a great person either), he cries his heart out over it. He has his own theory about men and women (one of his famous lines: girls are made of the substance of water, and boys are made of the substance of mud; when he's around girls he feels fresh and clean, but around men he feels like he's choking on foulness), and he's always drawn to hanging out with girls, not particularly caring what anyone else thinks.

On top of that, the way his mind works is sometimes genuinely mysterious. Everyone's watching an opera and he finds it boring, so he wanders off to the study to look at a painting of a beautiful woman, because he figures the woman in the painting must be lonely too and needs him to go comfort her. Or take his first meeting with Dai-yu: he asks whether she, like him, was born with a piece of jade. She says no, and he suddenly flies into a rage and bursts into tears, hurling the jade he was born with onto the floor. Reading that, I honestly thought, is this guy okay? But looking at it from another angle, I think Bao-yu is basically someone terrified of loneliness, with an unusually distinctive personality: as a man, he enjoys all the privileges that come with being male, and yet he doesn't like the traits typically expected of men, nor is he willing to follow the rules and social values that men have set up. Living under that kind of tension, it's no wonder his feelings erupt in such strange places.

I thought I didn't like Bao-yu, until his father, Jia Zheng, showed up, and I realized I couldn't stand Jia Zheng even more.

## Jia Zheng

The father-son relationship between Bao-yu and Jia Zheng is a constant, high-tension standoff. The two of them sit at opposite ends of a spectrum: Jia Zheng is a scholar, while Bao-yu can't stand studying; Jia Zheng is bound by countless rules, while Bao-yu's personality is wild and unrestrained. The two don't really interact much until Chapter 17, once Prospect Garden is finished being built. In that chapter, Jia Zheng, wanting to test his son's talent, takes him and a group of hangers-on to tour the garden and name its various scenic spots and pavilions. Before this, Bao-yu was always avoiding his father as much as possible, terrified of being quizzed on his studies, so I'd more or less assumed he was completely useless academically. But it turns out the names Bao-yu comes up with for the garden's spots are actually quite good, showing real literary talent, and yet his father is never satisfied. Not just unsatisfied, either: his father keeps calling him "beast, beast," and keeps flip-flopping on what he wants: when Bao-yu talks too much, he accuses his son of showing off in front of the hangers-on; when Bao-yu, stung by the scolding, stops talking, he snaps at him for staying silent. This man is genuinely exhausting and difficult to deal with.

Jia Zheng's rule-bound nature also comes through in his interactions with his daughter Yuan-chun. Yuan-chun, the eldest daughter, now an imperial consort, finally makes it home for a rare visit and speaks to her father from the heart, telling him that although she now lives in wealth and honor, being parted from her own flesh and blood leaves her, in the end, with no real joy in it. Jia Zheng responds with a long, relentlessly politically-correct speech, referring to himself as "your subject," and telling his daughter to serve the Emperor well so as not to squander such great imperial favor, and so on. Yuan-chun has no choice but to fend him off with equally formal, bureaucratic language in return. I suspect what she was really thinking was: I finally got off work, so why do I have to keep working the second I get home?

I think Jia Zheng is, underneath it all, a deeply repressed person, and that repression is exactly what keeps him at such a distance from his children. My guess is that Jia Zheng might actually envy Bao-yu, at least a little. In Jia Zheng's eyes, he himself has worked incredibly hard and lived an extremely "correct" life; meanwhile, his son is the type who was born extraordinary, gifted with talent, and doesn't have to try particularly hard at anything, and on top of that, his mother (Bao-yu's grandmother) dotes on him endlessly. The fact that Bao-yu's "incorrectness" still brings him such a comfortable, easy life might be exactly what throws Jia Zheng so off balance. Let's keep reading and see whether that guess holds up.

I've talked about the men for a while now, so let's move on to a woman I actually like. Having read the first twenty chapters, my favorite character by far is the one known as "Sister Feng," Wang Xi-feng.

## Wang Xi-feng
The moment Wang Xi-feng appears, she reads exactly like the type of extroverted older-sister figure who takes great care of her more introverted little sisters: she's warm and affectionate with Dai-yu right from the start. She's sharp-tongued and bold enough to call Grandmother Jia (her husband Jia Lian's grandmother) "Old Ancestress." She's resourceful enough to scheme up a plan to punish the lecherous Jia Rui after he tries to make advances on her. She also has a gentle, proactive, caring side: when her close friend Ke-qing falls ill, she's swamped with work and still makes time to visit and comfort her. She's also a textbook case of "the capable one gets stuck doing everything": not only does she run the day-to-day affairs of the Rong-guo mansion, but when trouble hits the Ning-guo mansion too, they call on her to handle that as well. It's no wonder that the narrator of *Dream of the Red Chamber*, describing Xi-feng, praises her as spirited and dashing, a model of refined elegance.

I've been thinking that part of why I like this character so much might just be that everyone else looks so hopeless by comparison, lol, and it really makes her competence stand out. Also, her personality reminds me of certain extroverted "aunties" and "big sisters" who showed up in my life back when I was much more introverted myself: they were like little suns; just having them around was enough to evaporate all my nervousness and awkwardness.

I really hope Wang Xi-feng gets a good ending! But something tells me she won't, ugh?

## The Mirror That's Impossible Not to Roast
The "Mirror for the Romantic" is, well, a mirror. It shows up in Chapter 12, lent to Jia Rui by a lame Taoist priest when Jia Rui is dying of illness.

The lame Taoist tells him that if he looks into the back of the mirror, his illness will be cured within three days, but under no circumstances should he look into the front. Naturally, Jia Rui doesn't listen. He looks into the back and sees a skeleton, and after the initial shock, he can't resist the temptation and looks into the front instead, and there's Wang Xi-feng, the woman he can't stop thinking about. He climbs right into the mirror to be with her, and it ultimately costs him his life.

I told this story to Sam, and he said, doesn't that sound exactly like one of those slightly useless gadgets from *Doraemon*? Or kind of like something Dr. Doofenshmirtz would invent, complete with a self-destruct button.

Fair point: this mirror really isn't very user-friendly. If the whole point is to save someone's life, why does it even need two sides? One side should be enough.

Sure, Jia Rui kind of brought it on himself, but I still think this "Mirror for the Romantic" gadget from the lame Taoist is honestly pretty useless too. I genuinely can't tell if he was trying to save people or destroy them.

## The Teetering Jia Household
I remember finishing Chapter 4 and jotting down a note: why does it already feel like this household is on the verge of collapse? A few reasons:
<br>
First, the family's young men run wild outside, and the elders just quietly clean up after them without ever really disciplining them.
<br>
Second, between the two Jia mansions, it somehow feels like Wang Xi-feng is the only woman actually holding things together.
<br>
Third, the household servants cause trouble and show no respect for their masters.
<br>
Fourth, when Yuan-chun, the consort living in the palace, comes home, she can't help but sigh and shake her head, telling her family not to be so extravagant.

## In Summary

Overall, there have been a few things about this book that genuinely surprised me.

I'd heard people say the web of relationships in *Dream of the Red Chamber* is incredibly complicated, but having actually started reading, I've found it's really not that hard to untangle. In Chapter 2, Cao Xueqin uses one of the characters to lay out a rough sketch of the entire family tree, and based on how he describes the relationships, you can actually go ahead and draft out a family tree for the Jia clan. Whenever a new character shows up later, the narrator is careful to clearly spell out how they're related to everyone else. Once you've sketched it out on paper, you're basically 70-80% of the way to understanding the whole thing.

On top of that, I originally assumed Cao Xueqin would save the Jia family's decline for much later in the story, but as it turns out, right from the very beginning, he's already been steadily laying the groundwork for a household full of cracks. I wonder where the next crack is going to show up. To find out what happens next, stay tuned for the next installment.

### [Fun Vocabulary]
*Dream of the Red Chamber* has some genuinely fun turns of phrase, so I wanted to collect a few here. Text in black is the book's own explanation; <span style="color: rgb(255, 106, 0);">orange text is my own take on the word.</span>
1. **Cuanduo (攛掇)**: to egg someone on, to instigate. <span style="color: rgb(255, 106, 0);">Both characters have the "hand" radical, like someone poking another person, prodding them into doing something.</span>
2. **Gousuiyaozhuo (詬誶謠諑)**: to insult and mock, to spread rumors and slander. Gou means to curse; sui means to scold; yao means rumor; zhuo means slander. <span style="color: rgb(255, 106, 0);">All four characters share the "speech" radical, so reading it feels like someone endlessly running their mouth.</span>
3. **"A hero among the rouge-and-powder set"**: <span style="color: rgb(255, 106, 0);">This is how Ke-qing describes Xi-feng. I find it delightful. I'd love to be a hero among the rouge-and-powder set myself.</span>
4. **"A hero with piercing eyes"**: refers to someone with sharp insight and outstanding intelligence. <span style="color: rgb(255, 106, 0);">A pretty funny phrase: it makes me think of the Cyclops from *The Odyssey*.![](/images/Post/49/2.jpeg)
</span>
