A while back, the name Sabrina Huang (黃麗群) kept popping up across a bunch of blogs I follow. At first I was a little contrarian about it: the more everyone talked about someone, the less I wanted to jump on the bandwagon. But then I saw the cover of *The Cat and I Don't Go Out*, with that plump cat curled up and rolled over showing its belly, and I just couldn't resist, so I borrowed the ebook and started reading.

As I read, I couldn't help but think of Professor Ko Yu-fen (柯裕棻): both of their writing is cool and sharp, occasionally faintly sweet, occasionally puncturing straight through the absurd emptiness of life. Genuinely similar in feel.

Then I read Huang's short story collection *The Room by the Sea* and saw that Ko had written the preface, and learned the two of them had been close friends for years. I couldn't help but blurt out, "That explains it!" Explains what, exactly? Explains why I caught a similar scent in both their writing, explains why I fell for Huang's prose in the first place. It all made sense.

If you already love Huang's essay collections, then I really, and I mean really, recommend you go read Ko Yu-fen's essay collections too. *Three Folds of the Primordial*, *A Sweet Instant*, and *A Hazy Adagio* are all fantastic.

I can just picture it: if Huang happened to stumble across this post of mine, she'd probably go dead silent for a moment, purse her lips, and say, "Who exactly are you recommending here?"

Bear with me a little, and let me use the reflections below to share what's been on my mind after a few days spent with your work.

---

Reading Huang's essays feels like spreading your fingers and dipping them into cold river water, pulling up glittering white sand and starfish with every scoop. So many of her sentences are unbelievably beautiful: **"The clouds, ground thin by sunlight, loll half-transparent along the mountain's edge or the corner of the sky."** (From *[Behind the Song](https://www.books.com.tw/products/0010584034?sloc=main)* · "Spring, Don't Come"). And her metaphors aren't there to show off vocabulary; they use concrete images to render abstract concepts, revealing how she really sees people and things. Take, for instance: **"What I truly want isn't for you to always see me as a jewel; what I truly want is to always be able to see you like a loaf of bread fresh out of the oven, steam still rising off it, giving off the smell of fire and sugar and wheat and butter."** (*Behind the Song* · "Fool's Gold"). To her, love isn't about making yourself precious and untouchable; it's about staying drawn, again and again, to the plain, beautiful, ordinary side of the person you love. Her writing kept making me wonder: how would I describe the people I truly treasure? I think what I want is to see someone the way you'd see a blanket fresh off the clothesline in the sun: able to leap into it without hesitation, curl up inside it, and breathe deep.
<br>

In the essay collection [*The Cat and I Don't Go Out*](https://www.books.com.tw/products/0010819316?sloc=main), my favorite piece is "Instructions for Liking Something," where every sentence pins down, with total precision, what it actually means to like something. After I finished it, one line refused to leave my head: **"Liking something is our last honesty, our last instinct, and our last integrity."** Why "last"? I think it's because if a person doesn't know what they like, they're already halfway to losing themselves. Living as a human being means not letting your life stay foggy and undefined; being able to clearly say what you like and don't like (not in a bratty, demanding-of-others way) is what lets you live with a bit more ease, a bit more certainty. And to get to that ease and certainty, you have to go exploring, digging as hard as you can, carving out a treasure cave where you can safely store everything you like and don't like.

Then, whenever the person you love shows up, you can take them on a full tour.

---

The short story collection [*The Room by the Sea*](https://www.books.com.tw/products/0010993922?sloc=main) wraps each story in a different skin: some are chilling, some are bleak. But underneath, they're all really the same thing: human desire, suppressed, twisted, and finally breaking through. Some of these stories look, on the surface, like something ripped from a lurid headline, and yet they don't let the reader stay a bystander, because Huang places you right up close to the character, close enough to inhabit, or feel, or observe someone wrapped entirely in sorrow, and to ask: what kind of person is this, really? When you're standing that close to someone, it becomes hard to judge them easily, to say their wrongdoing simply comes from a rotten heart, as if the rest of us were born with clean hearts, genetically set apart from them. These tragic characters, or, scaled up, so many people out in society, are, yes, sick, and badly so. But then again, do we ourselves, and the communities we belong to, not carry some gray patch of our own that no amount of treatment ever seems to heal?

I think trying to answer questions like that might be exactly the point of reading fiction. And as a reader having thoughts like these, does that mean I've, in some way, fulfilled what the novelist set out to do? Huang once wrote, in an essay, about what fiction-writing means to her: it's **"such a fine little sealed room for 'diagnosis': 'seeing' purely in the textual sense, with no intention of curing anything; it isn't a remedy for the heart, only a microscope."** (*[Things That Feel a Little Extravagant](https://www.books.com.tw/products/0010642773?sloc=main)* · "That Snake, That Illness"). I think that's exactly right: if a novelist's goal were to solve society's problems, they'd go into politics; if their goal were to solve the human heart's problems, they'd become a therapist. Maybe the real starting point of fiction is exposing a problem inside a safe, fictional world, letting people see its full complexity and feel real empathy for it, without having to bleed for it themselves.

---

And finally, one more push: go read Sabrina Huang's essays, her fiction, and her [blog](https://sabrinahuanglichun.wordpress.com/about/). And if you want to watch other people get roasted, or want to get roasted yourself, check out her [Do You Want to Get Roasted Too](https://www.youtube.com/@Soluluforurdelulu/videos).
