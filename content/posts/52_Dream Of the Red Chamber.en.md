## Preface
I've finally made it to chapter forty! Once you commit to a column, you've got to keep serializing it: I felt like if I let it sit too long I'd get lazy and lose the will to write, so I've been pushing myself to read a bit and write a bit, keeping pace. These twenty chapters delivered a lot of iconic scenes, including Dai-yu burying the fallen blossoms, and Grannie Liu's visit to Prospect Garden, the one that shows up in middle school textbooks. This time around, I want to talk about the two female leads, Lin Dai-yu and Xue Bao-chai.

### Quick recap of key plot points, Chapters 21-40
Bao-yu and his sisters and cousins move into Prospect Garden; Dai-yu buries the fallen blossoms; Jia Zheng nearly beats Bao-yu to death; a poetry club is formed; Grannie Liu visits Prospect Garden.

## Dai-yu
Sam asked me what the main plot of *Dream of the Red Chamber* actually is, and I said: it's just watching all these siblings fall in love! ("Watching a great family decline" would've been way too dry an answer, who'd want to read that!) Bao-yu and Dai-yu are basically the male and female leads of a romance drama: gorgeous, constantly falling into misunderstandings, sulking, finally patching things up only to suddenly realize the other person is their true soulmate, then endlessly testing each other's feelings all over again. If that's not a romance drama, what is?

Even though Cao Xueqin gives a fatalistic, predestined explanation for why Dai-yu cries so much, I want to first try understanding her tendency toward melancholy from a purely human angle. Of the three (Bao-yu, Dai-yu, and Bao-chai), Dai-yu is the youngest, Bao-yu is in the middle, and Bao-chai is the oldest. Dai-yu lost both her parents at a very young age (probably around middle-school age), and coming face to face with death is a life-altering thing for anyone, let alone a little girl her age. On top of that, her health is frail and she's frequently unwell; her own father died of illness. For her, death often feels like it's lurking right at the door, as if it could swallow her whole at any moment, so expecting her to be a cheerful, carefree kid is honestly not very realistic. Add to that the fact that she has to live with her uncle's entire extended household, which only means more friction and more chances to rub people the wrong way. She isn't especially confident in herself, and her personality tends toward the earnest and serious side, and life really is hard for her.

Watching this frail yet brilliantly talented girl in her tentative, blossoming romance with our wealthy young master, I couldn't help but let out an involuntary little chuckle of an "aunty smile" as a reader. Take this moment: Dai-yu hears that Bao-yu has been out visiting all day, and grows anxious, sending someone to check on him two or three times that evening; the moment she learns he's been scalded, she drops everything and hurries over herself. When the person you love gets hurt, Dai-yu's concern comes through loud and clear! And when Bao-yu drops by her room and overhears her talking to herself, Dai-yu, mortified at having let her feelings slip, blushes without meaning to, pulls her sleeve over her face, and turns to the wall, pretending to be asleep. Doesn't that just make you think Dai-yu is unbearably cute? And this same adorable Dai-yu even has a farm-girl look in her wardrobe! When she goes to bury the fallen blossoms, her outfit actually reminded me of Li Wei, the female lead from the drama *My Journey to You*, dressed head to toe like a farm girl, and yet somehow still coming across completely clean and utterly adorable.

Dai-yu and Bao-yu are clearly drawn to each other. So far, Dai-yu is the only one who can reach that place deep inside Bao-yu that aches over the passing of time and mourns the fading of spring, and she's also the only one who never once tells him to go work hard and chase after worldly success and status. That's exactly why Bao-yu loves her. Even though, deep down, they both quietly recognize each other as soulmates, the aimlessness of youth still keeps tangling them up. What makes things hard between them isn't just that they both tend to say the opposite of what they mean whenever things get the slightest bit romantic; it's also the far heavier weight of the fated "match of gold and jade."

Cao Xueqin never has the narrator directly explain what this "match of gold and jade" actually means. Instead, he lets the reader piece it together through the characters' own conversations: Bao-yu was born with a piece of jade in his mouth, and Bao-chai owns a gold locket, given to her by a monk, who told her that one day she would marry a young man born with jade. Everyone already knows this story, and everyone has quietly already assumed Bao-yu and Bao-chai will end up together. Because of this, for Dai-yu, even once she's certain that Bao-yu really is her soulmate, that sense of fatalism still keeps her from ever letting herself express her feelings for him without reservation, because every banquet eventually has to end, so maybe it's better not to have happy memories to lose in the first place. Reading this part, I understood a bit better why Bao-yu is always throwing his jade around and smashing it, because that "spiritual jade" feels like a "perfect match" he can never break free of or destroy, something everyone else thinks is wonderful, but which, for him, is really just a shackle trapping him away from what his heart actually wants.

So really, you can't blame Dai-yu for crying so much: she has her own reasons, and her own pain, that she just can't help.

## Bao-chai
As the second female lead of this romance drama, Bao-chai carries a maturity and worldliness well beyond her fifteen years. Like Dai-yu, she lost her father early, though her mother is still alive, so she's come to depend on the Jia household along with her mother and older brother, Xue Pan. Even though she's technically the younger sister, Xue Pan really doesn't act much like an older brother (he lazes around all day doing nothing), which has left Bao-chai's personality shaped more like an older sister's. And since she's also the eldest of the three among Bao-yu, Dai-yu, and herself, Bao-yu and Dai-yu end up feeling almost like her own younger brother and sister. On top of that, Bao-chai, like Dai-yu, is genuinely talented with words, but where Dai-yu leans romantic, Bao-chai's personality is more grounded and practical, much like her own room, which has almost no decoration and is entirely focused on function.

Bao-chai is extremely well-liked within the Jia household: her emotional intelligence and social skills are both remarkably high, and she has a real gift for being considerate of other people's feelings. She's sharply aware of how her own words and actions land on others. Once, when Grandmother Jia asks what she likes, Bao-chai, knowing full well that the old woman loves lively opera and sweet, soft food, tailors her answer accordingly, which only makes the old woman like her even more. Another time, she's about to go find Dai-yu, but notices Bao-yu has gotten there first, so she quietly turns and leaves instead, not wanting to make either of them uncomfortable. Bao-chai's habit of thinking of others comes as naturally to her as eating or drinking water. It never feels like she's deliberately trying to please anyone. If I had to name someone who deliberately tries to please people, I'd think of Jia Zheng instead: Cao Xueqin repeatedly uses the verb "smiling ingratiatingly" whenever describing Jia Zheng trying to curry favor with someone, but he never once describes Bao-chai that way.

Having read up through chapter forty, the question I find myself most curious about is this: does this endlessly considerate Bao-chai actually have feelings for Bao-yu? From the clues so far, it seems like she might just think of him as a little brother. As the female lead of the "match of gold and jade," she obviously knows about the prophecy, and yet her instinct is to actively keep her distance from Bao-yu. She even seems unimpressed when she notices Bao-yu's older sister Yuan-chun sent her the exact same gift as Bao-yu, and she sometimes teases Bao-yu and Dai-yu about their close, childhood-sweetheart-style bond. That said, when she overhears Bao-yu mumbling in his sleep, insisting he doesn't believe in the "match of gold and jade" but only in the "match of wood and stone," Bao-chai still visibly freezes up. My current guess is that she doesn't have romantic feelings for Bao-yu at all. She probably really does just see him as a little brother, and probably treats the whole "match of gold and jade" business as more of a "well, since everyone says so, I guess that's just how it is" kind of thing. She's not especially fond of the idea either, but there doesn't seem to be much need to actively refuse it, so as long as it keeps everyone happy, why not. I'm not sure if that guess is right or not. Let's keep reading and find out.


## Our Male Lead, Impossible Not to Roast
I keep feeling like this wealthy young master, Bao-yu, has no idea just how much trouble his behavior causes other people, much of the time.

For instance, there's a moment where he's caught in the rain outside and comes home to Green Delights soaking wet, wanting to change clothes, and the maid inside takes forever to open the door. So he decides that whoever it is, he's going to kick her the moment she opens up. Turns out it's Aroma, the maid who takes the best care of him out of anyone, and without stopping to think, he kicks her right in the ribs, hard enough that she coughs up blood. He does feel bad about it afterward and takes good care of her, but honestly, this guy is a bit much.

Then there's the incident where, without any sense of consequence, he flirts with Golden, his own mother's maid. His mother, protecting her son, decides the maid must be the one at fault and has her dismissed. Somehow, Golden can't get past it and ends up drowning herself in a well. Bao-yu, though upset, still turns around and asks Golden's sister, Silver, whether their mother is doing okay, genuinely oblivious to how the world works. He was the one who indirectly caused her death, and here he is asking about her mother's well-being.

And there's more! When Bao-chai complains about the heat and skips out on watching an opera with everyone else, Bao-yu's actual response is that no wonder people compare her to Lady Yang, since she's on the plump side too. This guy actually dares to say something that disrespectful to a woman. It's basically: "Oh, you feel hot? That must be because you're fat!" So rude!! 😡😡🤬 And it gets worse: when he catches sight of Bao-chai's snow-white arm, he can't help but think to himself that if that arm belonged to Miss Lin, he might get to touch it, but of all people it had to be hers, which just proves how unlucky he is. If that's not creepy, I don't know what is.

## In Summary
Reading *Dream of the Red Chamber*, it's pretty much unavoidable to end up either "Team Dai-yu" or "Team Bao-chai." Care to guess which team I'm on?

<div class="blur-text">Given how much enthusiasm I've clearly put into this, it's probably not hard to guess I'm Team Dai-yu! Dai-yu really is adorable, and even cuter when she's being prickly!

That said, even though I'm Team Dai-yu, Jia Bao-yu is really not my type at all. Sam is still my number one ❤️ (unprompted PDA, sorry).</div>

I think part of why I like her so much is that I see a version of my own middle-school self in her. Back then, I got emotional at the smallest things (okay, fine, I cried a lot), and I felt everything deeply when it came to life's partings and its underlying sadness, and I wasn't very good at processing complicated emotions, and I'd often get tangled up in my own head about things. But thankfully, I ran into some genuinely good people along the way (not that I never met bad ones, but the ratio was low), and, by some real stroke of luck, I made it through okay.

If I could ask for one thing, it would be for Dai-yu's story not to end in tragedy, because that feeling would be too much like watching a younger version of myself, missing just a few crucial lamps along the way, wander down a road that leads to ruin. So looking back, I really do feel grateful for the people who lit my way through those dim years of youth, and helped me find a different path.

Finally, it feels like a tragic fate is quietly closing in on all three of them: Bao-yu, Dai-yu, and Bao-chai. What they say and do keeps foreshadowing how it will all end: Bao-yu says that if Dai-yu dies, he'll become a monk; Dai-yu pushes Bao-yu away; and the poem Bao-chai writes seems to predict that her own marriage will be short-lived rather than lasting. How exactly do the three of them end up there? To find out what happens next, stay tuned for the next installment.


### [Fun Vocabulary]
Text in black is the definition, <span style="color: rgb(255, 106, 0);">orange text is my own take on it.</span>
1. Lieju (趔趄): to stagger, stumbling off-balance as if about to fall.
2. Daiyan ("dumb goose"): a mocking term for someone acting dim-witted, like calling someone a "fool" or a "dunce." <span style="color: rgb(255, 106, 0);">This phrase made me think of [that random goose](/posts/2026/07/cranes-and-goose/) I saw on the street a while back. Turns out, whether ancient or modern, everyone agrees geese just look kind of dopey.</span>
3. Futai: describes someone with a full, plump figure. <span style="color: rgb(255, 106, 0);">Fat.</span>
4. "The Idle Master of Wealth and Leisure": Jia Bao-yu's nickname (pen name). <span style="color: rgb(255, 106, 0);">The classical-era version of "rich and has way too much free time."</span>
5. Budangjiahuala (不當家花拉的): "budangjia" means "shouldn't" or "what a sin," and "huala" is a suffix, also said as "hualala" or "hualazi." <span style="color: rgb(255, 106, 0);">This construction follows basically the same logic as the modern phrase "well, that's it, we're cooked."</span>
